    Research

    Dark Halos, Loud Binaries: primordial black holes through the gravitational-wave lens

    I am a computational astrophysicist, and I ask a simple question: where do the binary black holes that gravitational-wave detectors observe come from? The LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA (LVK) catalog GWTC-5 now holds about 390 candidates, mostly binary black hole mergers. Their rate grows with redshift, their masses peak near 10 M⊙ and reach into the pair-instability gap where stars should leave no black hole, most have near-zero spin, and a few show signs of residual eccentricity. No single formation channel explains all of this. Some of these black holes may be primordial black holes (PBHs), formed in the early Universe and a candidate for the dark matter. Even if only ~0.1% of the dark matter were PBHs, they would merge at a rate comparable to what LVK observes, so the catalog can limit, or measure, that fraction. Doing so needs accurate PBH merger rates and a signature that separates PBHs from stellar black holes. That is what my work provides.

    Binaries in a crowded halo

    PBH binaries form before matter–radiation equality (z ≈ 3400). Earlier calculations assumed they then stay isolated until they merge. In reality, from z ≈ 12 onward most PBHs fall into dark matter halos, where encounters with other PBHs harden a binary (speeding up its merger), soften it, or break it apart. This leaves three merger channels: GW captures between single PBHs, binary–single interactions inside halos, and unperturbed binaries that stay outside halos.

    With I. Cholis, I built a numerical framework that includes all three channels, simulating millions of PBH binaries in growing halos of 104–1015 M⊙ and following each binary shell by shell. Binaries in low-mass halos and in the dense inner regions of large halos are hardened strongly, so this channel matters even if only ~1% of PBHs are in binaries.

    Aljaf & Cholis, PRD 111, 063020 (2025) · Aljaf & Cholis, PRD 113, 123001 (2026)

    Merger rates across cosmic time

    At the redshifts LVK observes (z ≲ 2), hardening inside halos raises the total PBH merger rate by about 50% over the standard unperturbed rate. I also corrected the analytical unperturbed rate by evolving large samples of binaries and counting only those still outside halos: they make up only ~20% of mergers at z ≈ 1 and ~10% today. GW captures are subdominant and come mostly from small halos. Unlike the astrophysical rate, the PBH rate keeps rising beyond z ≈ 2, which next-generation detectors can test.

    The rates for every channel are public on Zenodo.

    Aljaf & Cholis, PRD 113, 123001 (2026)

    Which halos host the mergers

    The binary–single rate depends on the halo: Milky-Way-sized halos (1012 M⊙) dominate today, while the smallest halos take over at high redshift and the most massive clusters contribute little there because they have not formed yet. The choice of halo mass function alone changes this rate by a factor of 6–8 up to z ≈ 1. For GW captures, I extended the rate from a single PBH mass to extended mass distributions: a lognormal distribution gives three times the monochromatic rate in a 1012 M⊙ halo.

    Aljaf & Cholis, PRD 111, 063020 (2025)

    How the rate scales

    The total rate scales as fPBH2 in all channels and linearly with the fraction of PBHs in binaries, except GW captures, which do not depend on it. With PBH mass, all channels scale as m−0.8 except GW captures, which are mass-independent for a single PBH mass. A lognormal mass distribution raises the rate by a factor of 1.28 over the monochromatic case. These scalings let the rates be applied to any PBH model.

    Aljaf & Cholis, PRD 113, 123001 (2026)

    Eccentricity: a fingerprint of primordial origin

    Masses alone cannot tell PBHs from stellar black holes, so I looked for a different signature. I evolved millions of 30+30 M⊙ PBH binaries from all channels, outside halos and in radial shells of five halos, and computed their eccentricity as they enter the bands of aLIGO, the Einstein Telescope (ET), Cosmic Explorer (CE), LISA and DECIGO. For fPBH = 3.4×10−3, ground-based detectors would see from about 60 (aLIGO) to 6–8×104 (ET, CE) PBH binaries, all effectively circular. But repeated hardening in dense halo cores keeps some binaries eccentric: LISA and DECIGO together would detect ~100 PBH binaries with e > 0.01. If they find none, the limits on fPBH improve by an order of magnitude.

    Aljaf & Cholis, arXiv:2607.23892 (2026)

    Reading the LVK catalog

    Using these merger rates, my collaborators and I set 95% upper limits of fPBH ~ 10−4–2×10−2 for PBHs of 5–80 M⊙ from LVK O1–O3. A follow-up fit to 153 O1–O4a events with first-generation, hierarchical and primordial populations prefers fPBH ≈ (3–8)×10−3 at 30–40 M⊙, but this preference depends on how the stellar channels are modeled. Both studies were led by Mehdi El Bouhaddouti.

    El Bouhaddouti, Aljaf & Cholis (2026) · El Bouhaddouti, Cholis & Aljaf (2026)

    Current work: the stochastic background

    I am now computing the stochastic gravitational-wave background from PBH binaries for masses of 0.01–100 M⊙, with monochromatic and lognormal mass distributions. I am extending my framework with one more channel, exchanges between PBH binaries and single PBHs, and will provide forecasts for current and future detectors.

    Earlier: dark energy and the Hubble tension

    During my Master's at the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC), I worked on the other half of the dark sector. I studied dark energy that interacts with dark matter using dynamical systems, reconstructed the expansion history of the Universe with Gaussian processes, and showed with Bayesian machine learning that f(T) gravity can ease the Hubble tension. I also worked on inflation.
