    Research

    Dark Halos, Loud Binaries: primordial black holes through the gravitational-wave lens

    Some black holes that gravitational-wave (GW) detectors have observed could be primordial black holes (PBHs), a dark matter candidate. Testing this idea requires robust calculations of their merger rate, so I built a numerical framework that follows PBH binaries from their formation in the early Universe to today, both inside and outside dark matter halos. I showed that dynamical interactions between PBHs inside dark matter halos increase the late-time merger rate by ~50% compared to the case when binaries are unperturbed in isolation. With these rates, I, with my collaborators, put one of the most robust limits on the fraction of dark matter in PBHs, fPBH ≲ 10−3–10−2 in the mass range 5–80 M⊙, and I showed that space-based detectors such as LISA and DECIGO could probe PBH binaries through their eccentricity.

    I am a computational astrophysicist, and I ask a simple question with deep consequences: where do the binary black holes (BBHs) that GW detectors observe come from? The current catalog of observed GW events, GWTC-5, which was released by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) collaboration, contains about 390 candidates, mostly BBH mergers. Their merger rate grows with redshift, roughly following star formation. Their mass distribution has a peak near 10 M⊙ and a change of slope near 35 M⊙, and it includes a number of black holes with masses in the pair-instability mass gap, where very massive stars should explode and leave no black hole. Their spin distribution shows that most of the black holes have near-zero spin, except a small group with spin magnitude χ ≈ 0.69. Moreover, a few events show evidence of non-zero residual eccentricity.

    These black holes may be astrophysical, where first-generation black holes from massive stars are paired in isolated binaries (the field binaries) or through dynamical encounters in dense star clusters, where the remnants can merge again (hierarchical mergers). Or they may be primordial (PBHs), born with almost zero spin in the early Universe and mostly paired into highly eccentric binaries. No single channel explains all of these features, so the observed population is most likely a mix of these channels, possibly including some PBHs. This matters for dark matter, whose nature remains unknown, and PBHs are one of the few candidates the LVK catalog can test in the stellar mass range. Even a fraction fPBH ~ 10−3 of dark matter in PBHs would give a merger rate comparable to the LVK rate, so the catalog can limit, or measure, fPBH. The important questions are then: What fraction of the observed BBHs are PBHs, and how do we disentangle them from the astrophysical populations, through which signatures?

    Binaries in a crowded halo

    Answering these questions requires accurate modeling of the formation and evolution of PBH binaries across cosmic time, including their dynamical interactions inside their host dark matter halos. Past works on the PBH merger rate assumed a monochromatic mass distribution and one channel, or assumed that binaries formed in the early Universe stay unperturbed until their merger, or treated the effect of the halos analytically. However, PBHs form in the radiation-dominated era with masses different from a monochromatic distribution, and they form binaries by matter-radiation equality (z ≃ 3400). More importantly, PBH binaries and single PBHs gradually start to fall into dark matter halos at z ≲ 12, where encounters with surrounding PBHs can change their orbital parameters (semi-major axis a and eccentricity e) and their merger time. This leaves three PBH merger channels, which depend on the PBH properties (mass distribution, initial a and e, fPBH, and fraction of PBHs in binaries) and also on the halo properties (mass growth, density profile, velocity dispersion of PBHs versus redshift, and the halo mass function).

    1. GW captures: two single PBHs pass close enough inside a halo that GW emission binds them.
    2. Binary-single interactions: binaries inside halos encounter other PBHs, which tighten them and speed up the merger (hardening), widen them and delay or prevent the merger (softening), or break them up (ionization). These interactions depend on both PBH and halo properties.
    3. Unperturbed binaries: binaries outside halos evolve only through GW emission and depend on the fraction of dark matter that stays outside dark matter halos.

    To account for all of these channels, with I. Cholis, I developed a numerical framework that accounts for all of these merger channels through simulations of millions of PBH binaries inside dark matter halos of 104–1015 M⊙.

    Merger rates across cosmic time

    Overall, dynamical hardening inside dark matter halos increases the total comoving merger rate of PBH binaries at late times (z ≲ 2) by ~50% compared to the case when they are assumed to be unperturbed. For GW captures, I expanded the rate of Bird et al. (2016) from a monochromatic PBH mass to extended mass distributions motivated by early-Universe formation, for halos of 104–1015 M⊙ up to z = 12: a lognormal distribution gives a rate three times higher than the monochromatic one in a 1012 M⊙ halo, but the contribution of the GW capture channel is subdominant and comes mostly from mergers in small halos. For binary-single interactions, I simulated large samples of PBH binaries in every dark matter halo: binaries in low-mass halos and in the inner regions of large-mass halos experience strong hardening, and this channel is important even if only 𝒪(10−2) of the PBHs are in binaries. The choice of halo mass function changes their rate by 6–8 times up to z ≈ 1. For unperturbed binaries, I corrected the analytical rate by evolving large samples of binaries via GW emission and counting only those still outside halos: they make up only ~20% of mergers at z ≃ 1 and ~10% today. The rates are public on Zenodo, and unlike the astrophysical rate, they keep rising beyond z ≈ 2.

    We also found that the rate scales with fPBH2 in all channels and scales linearly with the fraction of PBH binaries, except for GW captures, which are independent of it. Regarding the mass distribution, all channels scale as m−0.8 except GW captures, which do not depend on the PBH mass for a monochromatic distribution. For PBHs with a lognormal mass distribution, the rate increases by a factor of 1.28 compared to the monochromatic case in all channels (except GW captures).

    Reading the LVK catalog

    With M. El Bouhaddouti and I. Cholis, using our merger rates, we set 95% upper limits of fPBH ~ 10−4–2×10−2 in the range of 5–80 M⊙. We fitted the primary-mass and mass-ratio distributions of the LVK O1–O3 BBHs with a power-law astrophysical population plus PBH binaries from our three channels; the limits depend on the assumptions about the PBH mass distribution and binary fraction. Building on this, we fitted the masses and redshifts of 153 LVK O1–O4a events with first-generation black holes, hierarchical mergers, and PBHs: adding the PBH component substantially improves the fit, with fPBH ≃ (3–8)×10−3 at 30–40 M⊙. However, this preference depends on how we model the stellar channels, because hierarchical mergers also produce heavy black holes; to distinguish PBHs from stellar channels, we need a signature beyond the masses.

    El Bouhaddouti, Aljaf & Cholis (2026) · El Bouhaddouti, Cholis & Aljaf (2026)

    Eccentric echoes for space detectors

    As first author, I determined the orbital eccentricities of PBH binaries upon entering the bands of ground-based and space-based detectors. I evolved millions of 30+30 M⊙ PBH binaries from all channels, outside halos and, with a semi-analytic treatment, in radial shells of five halos, for aLIGO, the Einstein Telescope (ET), Cosmic Explorer (CE), LISA (ESA-led, with NASA), and DECIGO. For fPBH = 3.4×10−3, ground-based detectors would see about 60 (aLIGO) to (6–8)×104 (ET, CE) PBH binaries, all effectively circular, while repeated hardening encounters in dense halo cores keep some binaries eccentric: LISA and DECIGO together would detect 𝒪(102) PBH binaries with e > 0.01. If they find none, the limits on fPBH would improve by an order of magnitude.

    Current work

    The stochastic gravitational wave background of PBH binaries is my current work. I am computing it for PBH masses of 0.01–100 M⊙ both for monochromatic and lognormal PBH mass distributions, extending my numerical framework to include one more channel: exchanges between PBH binaries and singles. I will then provide forecasts for current and future detectors.

    Earlier: dark energy and the Hubble tension

    During Masters at University of Science and Technology of China in China, I worked on cosmology: dark energy and the H0 tension with Gaussian processes and Bayesian machine learning, and dark energy dynamics and inflation. I am the first author of seven of my ten papers and have given 18 talks, nine on this work. Since 2019, I have coordinated ICTP's Physics Without Frontiers in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, co-organizing two schools and a workshop.
